Relative Architecture Manifesto

Organizations deliver value by fulfilling responsibilities. These responsibilities exist within the business or product domain and are carried out through a combination of people, partners, processes, and software systems.

Traditional architecture approaches often begin by modeling systems or technology platforms. This perspective can obscure how work actually flows through the organization and can lead to architectures that reflect system boundaries rather than business responsibilities.

Relative Architecture proposes a different starting point: model the responsibilities that deliver outcomes across the ecosystem.

Responsibilities may be fulfilled in many ways. Some are performed manually by teams, some are handled by external partners, and some are implemented through software capabilities. Regardless of implementation, these responsibilities and their relationships should be represented in a unified model.

Responsibilities collaborate in two fundamental ways: by requesting outcomes and by sharing facts. Requests express intent, while facts are communicated through events. These interactions form the network through which work flows across the ecosystem.

Software capabilities represent the automation of specific responsibilities. Each capability owns the policies, vocabulary, and state required to fulfill its responsibility. Capabilities accept intents from requestors, may initiate intents toward dependencies, consume events from informers, and publish events for interested parties.

Responsibilities are organized into scopes such as ecosystems, domains, capabilities, and modules. These scopes help structure ownership and understanding, while the operational behavior of the organization emerges from the network of collaborations between responsibilities.

Because each responsibility follows the same interaction model, the architecture remains consistent regardless of scale. There is no single center of the system—only responsibilities collaborating through shared intents and events.

Relative Architecture encourages ecosystems that are:

Transparent, because responsibilities and their collaborations are visible

Autonomous, because each responsibility owns its policies and state

Composable, because collaboration occurs through clear contracts

Evolvable, because implementations can change without altering responsibilities

The goal is not to impose a rigid structure but to provide a clear lens for understanding and designing complex systems. By modeling responsibilities first and implementing them through capabilities, organizations can build ecosystems that evolve while remaining aligned with how the business actually operates.
